Last night, the price data was collected two hours late. The case is made up, but many of us have seen what happens next. Someone says at once: change the vendor. The choice is made before the facts are in. Someone patches the data by hand overnight, and next month it fails again: the symptom is gone, and the mechanism behind it is untouched. A meeting is held, everyone speaks, and in the end no one is able to decide or act. And someone asks an Agent for ten pages of analysis: there is more material, and neither the judgment nor the outcome is more reliable. Everyone keeps busy, yet no one can say who may decide what to do, which facts can be trusted, or which boundary must hold. The Data Department Work Guidelines are written against these four failures.

The guidelines are the department's contract for the quality of its work. They apply to its members, and to the Agents that members delegate work to. The value of data work is not a file acquired, a table produced, a report written or a process started. It lies in turning signals from the world into judgments, data assets and actions that others can rely on: explained, checked, and used within clear limits. So a task should achieve three things: resolve the problem; test the judgment and say when it holds; and leave the system able to recognize the next occurrence sooner, or handle it better. These are three outcomes, not three reports. The colleague who patched the data overnight achieved only the first. The guidelines help us keep facts, responsibility, action and evidence apart. They do not make every task fill out the same form.

Two kinds of authority need to stay apart. Authority to act answers who may decide what to do, and it has an order. Law, regulation, security requirements and mandatory company policy come first. Within those limits comes the explicit decision of the decision owner for the matter. Then contracts, policies, specifications and decision records in force. Plans, provisional agreements and personal preferences come last. Authority about facts answers what can establish what is true: primary records and repeatable observations, with a source, time, subject and scope. Second-hand accounts, caches kept for speed, generated views built from the records, Agent output and memory are not primary records, so they are only leads. Take last night. The collection ran two hours late, and the log records it: that is a fact. That the vendor published late is still a hypothesis, until someone checks the publication time. A hypothesis says what observation would refute it: if the publication time shows the vendor on time, the hypothesis fails. Whether to change vendors is a choice, and the decision owner for the matter makes it. The decision states its trade-off and the condition for revisiting it. The meeting at the start should have run like this. Before it, say what is to be decided, who decides, and which facts are needed. When it ends, reach a decision and settle who does what by when, or say what is still missing. Neither kind of authority stands in for the other. Authority to act cannot make a false fact true, and a fact does not grant permission to act. When the two conflict, report the conflict and its impact. Don't alter the record, and don't silently act as if one side had won.

Four boundaries must never be crossed. First, tell the truth: no fabrication, no hiding, no cherry-picking, no uncertainty dressed as certainty. Second, stay in bounds: respect the limits of subject and time, competence and permission, data, security, compliance and action. Third, name the owner: every important judgment, decision, change and acceptance has an accountable person. Fourth, do not claim false completion: without current evidence matching the claim, call nothing complete, correct, usable or adopted. Inside them, autonomy, exploration and creative work are welcome. When an action would cross one, stop, make it visible, and escalate. If one has already been crossed, stop the affected action, disclose it, correct it as far as you are permitted, and escalate according to the risk. Risk sets the process. Local, reversible work with no external commitment can close in one clear exchange that states the conclusion, basis and action. Never downplay work to skip the record it needs. Cross-role or extended work, or work with competing options or material uncertainty, needs the problem, options, execution path and acceptance written into the existing work record. Work touching production, funds, sensitive data, security or compliance, deletion or overwrite, an external commitment or anything irreversible also needs a written decision, explicit authorization, a rollback or degradation path, independent review and human acceptance. Last night's case belongs here, whether the fix is a new vendor or a change to the collection job in production. Independent review examines the actual facts, reasoning, changes and evidence, not the author's or the Agent's own account of them. Whatever the risk, every duty still applies.

Calling data usable takes evidence too. A file that opens, a chart that looks good, a promising model signal: none of them alone establishes meaning, quality, permission or reproducibility. Data earns trust only after it is shown fit for a specific use. So before relying on it, ask six questions. Where did it come from? When did it become available? What does it mean? What changed it? How good is it? And how may it be used? Data that cannot answer them may be explored within its authorized scope, but stays out of shared, production and decision work until it is shown fit for that use. Last night's prices reached us two hours later than usual, so any backtest of those hours must use that later time. The data patched in overnight needs its history too: who added it, and from where. In historical research, distinguish what could have been known then from what can be seen in hindsight. If the vendor later corrected its prices, today's corrected series cannot show what was knowable at the time. Reports, catalogs, caches and Agent summaries are all derived from other data, and none may quietly become the source of truth.

Back to last night. The publication time and the job log show the vendor was on time and our own job started late. The fix has three layers. First, fill in the missing data to contain the damage. Then start the job earlier to repair the direct cause. Last, stop it happening again: work out why the existing system did not prevent or catch the delay in time, and add the lightest safeguard the risk calls for. Doing only the first two is not a systemic fix. The decision owner approves an earlier start and sets its limits. The job is changed only within them. Current runs give the evidence. The claim stays within that evidence. Then come acceptance and learning. This is a chain of trust: lower risk means less process, but the chain cannot skip a link. Last night's late run stays on the record; later runs that start on time do not erase it. Report the state, not the effort. "Actively progressing" and "almost done" say nothing about state. Verified means the checks passed for a stated subject, version, environment and limit. Published at the agreed destination does not mean anyone uses it; to claim use, say what use was actually observed. Every completion claim answers the same questions. What is claimed, about which subject and version? Verified when, by whom, and how? Covering what, and leaving what unproved? When evidence is missing, narrow the claim rather than inflate the wording. A passing test is not acceptance. And an Agent reporting completion does not make the work complete.

In human–AI collaboration, Agents work within a delegated boundary to extend what people can investigate, reason about and do: search, analysis, drafting, implementation, testing and review. But an Agent is not a source of organizational authorization, and it cannot make commitments on people's behalf. Direction, authorization, decisions and responsibility for consequences stay with people. Before the ten pages at the start were written, someone should have said which decision they were meant to support. Collecting information is not the goal. So delegate with a clear boundary, not a pile of context. A consequential delegation states its purpose and scope, the deliverable, the limits and permissions, how the result will be accepted, and when to stop. Telling an Agent to "just handle it" does not set an authorization boundary. A delegation for last night might read: "To help decide whether to change vendors, find last night's publication time and the time our job started. Work read-only. Say where each time came from and what you could not verify. Stop and ask me before changing the schedule." The person who calls an Agent owns its context, permissions, verification and result. Submitting Agent-assisted work means you have understood and checked it, and accept responsibility for its consequences. Delegation transfers the work, not that duty.

This kind of delay can recur, so before calling it fixed, leave a check behind: a monitor on the schedule that warns the moment the agreed time passes and the data has not arrived. The aim of learning is to find the next failure earlier, judge it more easily, and need less manual rescue, not to increase the file count. There may have been early signs: a few minutes late now and then, someone stepping in again and again to rescue the run. Look at their pattern and impact before deciding what to do about them. But a safeguard that is needed must not wait for an incident, or for a department rule. Before a new practice becomes a department rule, it needs an observed problem, a material risk with evidence, or an obligation that applies; then a small trial, an owner, and evidence of net benefit. A rule that has lost its subject, has no user, duplicates a source of truth, or costs more than it returns should be retired. The monitor, too, needs an owner, and a later look at whether it really caught problems earlier. Back to the price data we began with. Its next user needs clear meaning, the facts available at the time, evidence that matches each claim, and a permitted, bounded use. And when you convey a judgment to someone else, be faithful first, then clear, then elegant.
